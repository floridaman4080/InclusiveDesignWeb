3.2 Exploring User Experience

Character Profiles

Pain Points

Low contrast, glare, and disconnected tactile routes hinder safe navigation.

Design Guidance

Prioritize high-contrast cues and continuous tactile support.

4.1 Visual Cues

Visual cues: clear, simple, and not used alone

Visual information was common — signs, maps, arrows, labels, warning colors.

“Strong contrast at the edge of steps helps friends with low vision move faster, while weak contrast makes them stop and ask for help.”

Key Insight

For low-vision users, high contrast, large signs, clear edges, stable lighting, and reduced glare can make routes easier to notice.

4.2 Tactile Cues

Tactile cues: continuous, connected, unblocked

Touch was the most direct source of information. The white cane detected information before the body moved forward.

“When the tactile line stops in the middle of the path, I feel lost because I do not know if I made a mistake or the design did.”

Key Insight

Tactile design should be a route system, not an isolated strip. A blocked tactile route creates more risk than none.

4.3 Vision and Touch

The most effective design occurs when visual and tactile information overlap

Visual

Contrast, lighting, signs, edges

Mutual Confirmation
Tactile

Cane feedback, surface texture, route continuity

While visual cues accelerate navigation for low-vision users, tactile feedback ensures routes are confirmable for blind individuals.

5.1 Discussion – Design Implications

Public-space cues should support real decisions

01

Entrance and Orientation

Clear entry cues support the first decision. Thresholds, signage, and route information help users enter or continue.

02

Crossing and Direction

Stable details help users cross safely. Surface changes, crossings, and directional cues reduce uncertainty.

03

Level Change and Guidance

Visual and tactile cues should work together. Stairs, ramps, and route guidance support safe continuation.

6 Conclusion

Inclusive design works best when visual and tactile cues confirm each other

For the visually impaired, inclusive design works best when clear and consistent visual cues are reinforced by continuous tactile guidance and other nonvisual information, enabling users to better understand spaces, assess risks, and navigate confidently toward real destinations.
