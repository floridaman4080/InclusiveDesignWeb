1.1Introduction – Inclusive Spaces

![Slide 6 Image 7](ppt-content_files/slide_06_image_07.png)

- Public spaces should be safe, clear, and usable for people with different levels of vision. However, visual impairment is a wide category. Some people are blind, while many others have low vision and can still use contrast, light, shape, and distance cues.
- Inclusive design should not only respond to the smallest group at the top of the pyramid. It should create public spaces that work across this wider range of visual ability.

## Slide 17

4.1 Visual cues: clear, simple

![Slide 17 Image 20](ppt-content_files/slide_17_image_20.jpg)

Visual information was common — signs, maps, arrows, labels, warning colors.

Research Challenges

- "strong contrast at the edge of steps helps friends with low vision move faster, while weak contrast makes them stop and ask for help."
- — Interview participant

![Slide 17 Image 21](ppt-content_files/slide_17_image_21.jpg)

- KEY INSIGHT
- For low-vision users, high contrast, large signs, clear edges, stable lighting, and reduced glare can make routes easier to notice.

Fig. 7 — Elizabeth Line: cooler light in transitions, warmer in wayfinding spaces

## Slide 18

4.2 Tactile cues: continuous, connected, unblocked

![Slide 18 Image 22](ppt-content_files/slide_18_image_22.png)

Touch was the most direct source of information. The white cane detected information before the body moved forward.

- "When the tactile line stops in the middle of the path, I feel lost because I do not know if I made a mistake or the design did."
- — Interview participant

![Slide 18 Image 23](ppt-content_files/slide_18_image_23.png)

- KEY INSIGHT
- Tactile design should be a route system, not an isolated strip. A blocked tactile route creates more risk than none.

Fig. 8 — Hazelwood School: tactile wall and continuous route

## Slide 19

4.3 The Relationship Between Vision and Touch

![Slide 19 Image 24](ppt-content_files/slide_19_image_24.png)

While visual cues accelerate navigation for low-vision users, tactile feedback ensures routes are confirmable for blind individuals.

![Slide 19 Image 25](ppt-content_files/slide_19_image_25.png)

- KEY INSIGHT
- The most effective inclusive design occurs at decision points where visual and tactile information seamlessly overlap and confirm one another.

Figure 9. Enabling Village’s integrated “Enable Lanes” featuring high-contrast visual pathways and synchronized tactile floor indicators

## Slide 23

6 Conclusion

![Slide 23 Image 27](ppt-content_files/slide_23_image_27.png)

For the visually impaired, inclusive design works best when clear and consistent visual cues are reinforced by continuous tactile guidance and other nonvisual information, enabling users to better understand spaces, assess risks, and navigate confidently toward real destinations.
